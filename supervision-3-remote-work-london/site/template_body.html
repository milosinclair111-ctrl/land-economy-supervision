Has remote work made London more dominant?

A step-by-step answer: build Krugman's model, add remote work, then test it against ten years of ONS data.

Milo Sinclair · Paper 7, Regional Economics and Policy · Supervision 3 · November 2026
STEP 1 · THE QUESTION AND THE TENSION

Remote work pulls London two ways at once

In Krugman's (1991) core–periphery model (Lectures 7–8), firms want to be near their customers because selling at a distance costs money, and workers move to whichever region pays the higher real wage. That creates a snowball: a region with a few more workers has more local spending, so more firms want to be there, so it pays more, so more workers come.

Remote work changes the model in two opposite ways:

  1. It lowers trade costs (T) for services. A London firm can serve a client in Leeds over video instead of keeping an office there. Cheaper trade strengthens the snowball. More London.
  2. It lets people earn in one region and spend in another. Someone can work for a London firm and live in Kent. I add this to Krugman's model as θ, the share of each region's wages spent in the other region. It drains the snowball, because the big region no longer gets all its workers' spending. Less London.

Try both below. Lower the trade cost and the workers pile into one region. Raise θ and they spread out again.

Orange dots are workers who can move; grey dots are farmers who stay put, half in each region.

How the model works

The model is Krugman (1991) in the textbook form of Fujita, Krugman and Venables (1999, ch. 5). For any split of mobile workers between the regions (λ in region 1), four equations give each region's income, prices and wages:

Income:  Y₁ = μ[(1−θ)·λ·w₁ + θ·(1−λ)·w₂] + (1−μ)/2
Price index:  G₁ = [λ·w₁^(1−σ) + (1−λ)·(T·w₂)^(1−σ)]^(1/(1−σ))
Wage:  w₁ = [Y₁·G₁^(σ−1) + Y₂·G₂^(σ−1)·T^(1−σ)]^(1/σ)
Real wage:  ω₁ = w₁·[(1−θ)·G₁^(−μ) + θ·G₂^(−μ)]

Region 2 is the mirror image. The only change I made to Krugman is θ: a share θ of each region's wages is spent in the other region, so it enters the income and real-wage equations. With θ = 0 it is exactly Krugman's model.

These equations cannot be solved by hand, because each region's wage depends on the other's. So the computer guesses the wages, plugs them in, gets a slightly better answer, and repeats until the answer stops changing. Then, about sixty times a second, it works out the real wage in each region, moves a few workers towards the region that pays more, and redraws the dots.

Two numbers are held fixed at standard textbook values: σ = {{sigma}} (how different the products are from each other) and μ = {{mu}} (the share of spending on these goods and services). As a check, with θ = 0 my code finds the textbook tipping points: the break point at T = {{break0}} and the sustain point at T = {{sustain0}}, matching the published formulas to seven significant figures.

What I can measure

Nobody publishes "the trade cost of selling services", so I cannot measure T directly. But I can measure two things:

  • The net result: did remote-able jobs actually move towards London? That is the outcome of both forces together.
  • The leak (θ): did more of London's pay start going to people living outside London?

If jobs moved into London even though θ rose, the trade-cost force must have been the stronger one.

STEP 2 · HOW BIG DOES EACH FORCE NEED TO BE?

Mapping the trade-off

Step 1 shows the two forces pull opposite ways. To see how much of one it takes to cancel the other, I ran the same model for every combination of the two: 84 values of the trade cost T (from 1.01 to 2.2) times 45 values of θ (from 0 to 0.45), which is 3,780 runs. σ and μ stay fixed. For each run the computer asks two questions: can an even split last, and can one big cluster last?

The edge of the orange area is the trade-off. With θ = 0 a cluster survives at trade costs up to {{sustain0}}; with θ = 0.1 only up to {{sustain10}}; with θ = 0.2 only up to {{sustain20}}. So every 10 points of θ has to be matched by a {{fall10b}}–{{fall10a}}% fall in trade costs for the cluster to survive. I use this in Step 9.

"One cluster" means all the mobile workers in one region; the farmers stay split, so the cluster holds about 70% of everyone. In Krugman's basic model, in-between splits like 60/40 are not stable: they tip one way or the other. Real London obviously isn't all-or-nothing, so I use the model for which way the forces push, not where Britain ends up. As the lecture slides say, do not take the results literally.

STEP 4 · THE DATA

What happened to London's share of each industry?

I downloaded the ONS Business Register and Employment Survey (BRES), which counts jobs at each workplace, for every industry in every region of Great Britain from 2015 to 2024. For each industry I asked one question: what share of Britain's jobs in it are in London, and did that share go up or down after 2020? I compare the 2015–19 average with 2022–24, leaving out the lockdown years 2020–21.

Which industries count as remote-able

I sort industries by how much their staff work from home, using the ONS Business Insights and Conditions Survey (December 2023): the share of businesses where staff were working hybrid or fully from home.

STEP 5 · THE TEST

Did remote-able jobs move to London more than in-person jobs?

The result

London's share of jobs can change for all sorts of reasons. So rather than just asking whether its share of remote-able jobs rose, I compare it with its share of in-person jobs, which remote work shouldn't affect. Anything that moved both cancels out. The worry: were they rising anyway?

If finance and tech were already moving to London before 2020, the "effect" is just an old trend carrying on. In this data they weren't. What's left

Allowing for trends makes the result bigger, not smaller, because remote-able industries were, if anything, drifting slightly away from London before 2020. But the estimate is noisier, so it is still borderline. The honest conclusion: London's share of remote-able jobs rose after 2020, and the timing points to remote work, but the evidence falls just short of proof.

STEP 6 · OTHER CHECKS

Could it be a fluke?

Would the test find something even if nothing happened?
I ran a fake version: I pretended remote work started in 2017 and used only 2015–19, when there was no remote-work shock. If the method were biased towards finding a London gain, it would find one here too. It doesn't: the fake "effect" is {{placebo_effect}}%, the opposite direction, so remote-able jobs were not already drifting towards London.
The method doesn't manufacture a London gain.

STEP 7 · WHERE DID THE JOBS COME FROM?

It depends where you draw London

Lecture 1 says a region should be a functional area, where people live and commute. London's commuting area spills well into the South East and East of England. So I redrew the boundary and reran the same test.

Each tile is a region, coloured by how much its share of Britain's remote-able jobs changed after 2020 (percentage points). Orange gained, blue lost. Hover for numbers.

London gained {{london_chg}} points. The South East and East lost {{belt_loss}} between them. Add the three together and the wider London region barely moved ({{gse_chg}}). So London did gain on the rest of Britain, but by taking jobs from its own commuter belt, not from the North or the Midlands. This is the strongest result in the project. As the Lecture 9–10 slides put it, boundaries can move the answer more than the economy does.

One caution: the survey records home-based staff at the office that manages them. If a firm closed its Reading office and its staff now work from home but report to London, those jobs show up as London jobs though nobody moved. Some of this shift may be on paper.

STEP 8 · THE SECOND FOOTPRINT

Measuring θ: how much of London's pay leaves London?

The ONS Annual Survey of Hours and Earnings (ASHE) reports London pay two ways: by where the job is, and by where the worker lives. The difference is pay earned in London by people who live outside it, who mostly spend it where they live. Here "London" is the official London region, so "outside" mostly means commuters from Kent, Surrey and Essex.

The share going to non-residents averaged {{leak_pre}}% in 2015–19 and {{leak_post}}% in 2022–25, a jump of about {{leak_jump}} points that arrives in 2022, when hybrid working settled. That is the second force showing up.

One caveat: after the 2021 Census the ONS cut its estimate of London's population, and that feeds into how the survey scales up London residents. That makes the residents' pay total a bit smaller and the leak look a bit bigger. I still use the data, for three reasons: it is the best official measure available; the direction matches other evidence (ONS data showing hybrid working has stuck, and the "donut effect" in other cities); and my main conclusion comes from the jobs data, so it does not depend on the exact size of the leak.

STEP 9 · BACK TO THE MODEL

Which force was stronger?

θ rose by about {{leak_jump}} points. From the map in Step 2, a {{leak_jump}}-point rise in θ is cancelled out by a fall in trade costs of only about {{fall3}}%, and remote technology plausibly cut the cost of serving distant clients by more than that. So the model predicts London stays the cluster, which fits the jobs data: remote-able jobs moved towards London despite the leak. The trade-cost force was the stronger one.

The model can't see Step 7, though. It has only two regions, so it can't tell "London gained from the North" apart from "London gained from Reading". That is Martin's (1999) criticism of these models: their regions are not real places.

STEP 10 · THE ANSWER

So, has remote work made London more dominant?

Yes, a little, but mostly at the expense of its neighbours. London's share of Britain's remote-able jobs rose from {{remote_pre}}% to {{remote_post}}% while its share of in-person jobs barely moved, and the pay leak is too small to undo that. But the gain came from London's own commuter belt rather than the rest of the country, and the main test is borderline rather than conclusive. So remote work probably helped London, without widening the gap between the London area and the rest of Britain.

In Krugman's terms, both forces showed up. The trade-cost force (firms concentrating) was stronger than the spending force (wages spent elsewhere), so the cluster got stronger. But they act at different scales: firms concentrate in the centre while workers and their spending spread across the region. This matches Ramani and Bloom's "donut effect" in US cities, where activity left city centres for their suburbs rather than for other cities.

Limits, and what would settle it

  • Only three years of post-2020 data. Davis and Weinstein (2002) found that even huge shocks to cities can fade.
  • I can't separate remote work from the tech and AI boom, or the within-region shift from the Elizabeth line.
  • Groups are by industry, not job: a bank's cleaners count as remote-able.
  • To settle it: firm-level data comparing similar firms with different remote-work policies, tracking where staff live as well as where they are recorded.
HOW THIS WAS BUILT

From raw data to answer

All data is official ONS data from the Nomis API: BRES (dataset NM_189_1), ASHE by workplace and by residence (NM_99_1, NM_30_1). Python scripts download the data, run the model, the tests, the checks and the boundary test, and write the results to files; a final script builds this page from those results, so every number here comes straight from the analysis. The code is on GitHub. I used an AI assistant to help write the code.
